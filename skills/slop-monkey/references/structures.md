# Structural patterns to inspect

A structural pattern becomes slop when it substitutes a familiar shape for precise thought. Preserve structures that express a real contrast, sequence, or cadence; revise those that manufacture drama or repeat mechanically.

## Canned contrast and reveal

Common templates include:

- “Not because X. Because Y.”
- “X isn't the problem. Y is.”
- “The answer isn't X. It's Y.”
- “It feels like X. It's actually Y.”
- “The question isn't X. It's Y.”
- “It stops being X and starts being Y.”
- “Not just X, but also Y.”

When X exists only as a runway, state Y directly. Keep the contrast when readers may reasonably believe X and distinguishing it from Y is part of the argument. Name the evidence for the distinction.

## Negative runway

Patterns such as “It wasn't X. It wasn't Y. It was Z.” delay the claim through elimination. Start with Z unless the discarded possibilities matter to the reasoning. If they matter, explain why evidence rules them out rather than using repetition for suspense.

## Dramatic fragmentation

Watch for stacked fragments and one-line revelations:

- “Speed. Quality. Cost.”
- “That's it. That's the lesson.”
- “This unlocks something. Trust.”
- “X. And Y. And Z.”

Join fragments when punctuation is carrying more emphasis than the content. Retain a fragment when the surrounding voice supports it and repeated use has not made the rhythm mechanical.

## Rhetorical setup

These constructions can announce an insight before delivering it:

- “What if I told you…?”
- “Here's what I mean:”
- “Think about it.”
- “And that's okay.”
- a question followed at once by the writer's answer

Lead with the insight when the setup adds no context. A genuine question can remain when the document invites the reader to decide or when the answer is not predetermined.

## Hidden agency

Abstract nouns and passive constructions can obscure who acted:

| Hidden actor | More specific form |
| --- | --- |
| “The decision emerged.” | “The product lead chose the smaller scope.” |
| “A complaint became a fix.” | “The support team reproduced the complaint, and an engineer fixed it.” |
| “The conversation moved toward pricing.” | “Maya redirected the discussion to pricing.” |
| “The data tells us…” | “The researchers interpreted the survey as…” |
| “Mistakes were made.” | “We shipped the wrong configuration.” |

Name the actor when responsibility, causality, or evidence matters. Passive voice remains useful when the actor is unknown, intentionally irrelevant, or less important than the receiver: “The samples were stored at 4°C.”

Inanimate subjects can act in literal, conventional ways. “The report shows a decline” is often clearer than adding a human interpreter. Fix false agency, not every nonhuman subject.

## Narrator at a distance

Generalizations such as “People tend to…,” “Nobody designed this,” or “This happens because…” can hide missing evidence. Choose the form the evidence supports:

- name the observed group;
- describe a concrete scene;
- address the reader when the genre already speaks to them;
- identify the person or process responsible.

Avoid forcing second person into academic, legal, technical, or third-person prose whose genre calls for distance.

## Mechanical rhythm

Inspect sequences with:

- several sentences of similar length and syntax;
- repeated three-item lists;
- every paragraph ending in a short verdict;
- repeated em dashes or parenthetical asides;
- multiple paragraphs beginning with the same connector;
- stacked short sentences used for emphasis.

Revise enough of the sequence to restore natural cadence. A three-item list, em dash, or short ending may remain when it fits the syntax and occurs by choice rather than habit.

## Template-shaped presentation

A response can be readable yet still feel generated when every idea arrives in the same container. Inspect:

- a heading for every short paragraph;
- repeated bold-label-and-colon bullets;
- symmetrical sections whose content does not need equal weight;
- a summary that repeats the introduction;
- title-case headings in conversational writing;
- a closing “next steps” section with no necessary next step;
- tables used where two sentences would be easier to read.

Choose structure from the reader's task. Keep headings that help navigation, lists that support scanning, and tables that make comparison easier. Let a short answer remain short.

## Sterile neutrality

Mechanical balance can hide the judgment the reader needs. Common forms include equal lists of pros and cons when one side is decisive, recommendations that avoid choosing, and conclusions that repeat evidence without interpreting it.

State the supported judgment and its trade-off. Preserve genuine uncertainty, but do not manufacture neutrality when the evidence points in one direction.

## Sweeping authority

Words such as “every,” “always,” “never,” “everyone,” and “nobody” often claim more than the evidence supports. Replace them with the measured scope, named population, or actual frequency. Keep an absolute when the rule or evidence is absolute.

## Formulaic narrative transitions

Templates such as “By the time X, I was Y,” “the real problem,” and “what happened next changed everything” can make unrelated writing sound alike. Replace the template with the event, elapsed time, or consequence that matters. Preserve it when it is natural to the writer's voice and not part of a repeated pattern.
