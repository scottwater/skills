# Phrase patterns to inspect

These phrases often signal filler, canned emphasis, or abstraction. Diagnose the sentence before editing it. Delete a phrase when it adds no meaning; replace it when the sentence needs a more precise claim; keep it when the genre or writer's voice gives it a real function.

## Throat-clearing

These openings announce a point instead of making it:

- “Here's the thing”
- “Here's what…” / “Here's why…”
- “It turns out”
- “The uncomfortable truth is”
- “The reality is”
- “Let me be clear”
- “I'm going to be honest”
- “Can we talk about…”
- “At its core”
- “When it comes to…”
- “In today's…” / “In a world where…”

Test the sentence without the opener. If the remaining clause stands on its own, begin there.

## Manufactured emphasis

These phrases often tell readers how strongly to react:

- “Full stop” / “Period”
- “Let that sink in”
- “Make no mistake”
- “This matters because…”
- “Here's why that matters”
- “The stakes are high”
- “The consequences are real”
- “I promise”

Replace the instruction to care with the fact or consequence that earns attention.

## Meta-commentary

These phrases narrate the document's structure instead of advancing it:

- “Let me walk you through…”
- “In this section, we'll…”
- “The rest of this essay explains…”
- “As we'll see…”
- “I want to explore…”
- “Hint:”
- “Plot twist:” / “Spoiler:”
- “But that's another post”

Keep navigation only when readers need it to use a long or complex document.

## Business shorthand

Prefer a plain verb or concrete noun when shorthand hides the action:

| Inspect | Possible replacement |
| --- | --- |
| navigate challenges | handle the named problem |
| unpack | explain, examine |
| lean into | accept, use, commit to |
| landscape | market, field, situation |
| game-changer | name the measured effect |
| double down | invest, repeat, increase |
| deep dive | analysis, review |
| take a step back | reconsider, review |
| moving forward | next, from now on |
| circle back | return to, revisit |
| on the same page | agree on the named decision |

Technical and domain terms can be the clearest wording for an expert audience. Replace jargon only when a more exact phrase exists.

## Empty modifiers

Inspect softeners and intensifiers such as:

- really, very, just
- actually, literally
- genuinely, honestly, truly
- deeply, fundamentally, inherently
- inevitably, importantly, crucially
- simply, clearly, obviously
- interestingly, notably

A modifier earns its place when it changes scope, degree, certainty, or meaning. Otherwise, cut it or supply the evidence it was standing in for. Do not remove every adverb by form; “rotate clockwise” and “legally required” carry information.

## Vague importance claims

These constructions claim significance without specifying it:

- “The reasons are structural.”
- “The implications are significant.”
- “This is the deepest problem.”
- “This is what leadership looks like.”
- “This actually matters.”

Name the mechanism, affected person, magnitude, or consequence. If none is available, the claim may not belong.

## Stock self-awareness

These phrases can imitate intimacy without creating it:

- “You already know this, but…”
- “X is a feature, not a bug.”
- “dressed up as…”
- “creeps in”
- “They exist, I promise.”

Keep a familiar phrase only when it is the most accurate wording and fits the established voice. Familiarity alone is not emphasis.
